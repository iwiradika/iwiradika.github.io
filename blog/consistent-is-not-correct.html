Consistent Is Not Correct

Four papers from NCME’s AI in Measurement and Education Conference tested large language models on Q-matrices, item difficulty, and rater rationales. Read together, they show three properties that are easy to confuse: stability, agreement, and validity.

I Nyoman Indhi Wiradika
Published October 10, 2026
9 min read

NCME held its second AI in Measurement and Education Conference (AIME-Con) in Pittsburgh on October 5–7, 2026. The proceedings are already open on the ACL Anthology: 77 full papers and 60 works in progress. Many of them use a language model to produce something that later enters a measurement model, such as an item-by-skill map, a difficulty estimate, or a score with its justification.

That use raises a measurement question that comes before any question about speed or cost. If the LLM output feeds a psychometric model, the output is part of the instrument. It needs the same scrutiny we give to an expert panel or a human rater.

Three Checks That Answer Different Questions

The papers become easier to read once we separate three properties. A procedure is stable if it gives the same result when repeated. It shows agreement if different models or runs reach the same answer. It is valid for our purpose if its result matches an external criterion that we trust, such as an expert Q-matrix, calibrated item parameters, or a human rater’s reasoning. Each check can pass while the next one fails.

1. The Same Model Rebuilt the Q-Matrix and Reclassified Most Students

A Q-matrix states which skills each item requires. In cognitive diagnosis, it determines how responses become a mastery profile for each student. Several recent studies ask an LLM to draft the matrix from item text, then report one run or a majority vote across runs.

Minkwon Kim (Teachers College, Columbia) used the 23 released TIMSS 2011 grade-8 mathematics items, a published seven-attribute expert Q-matrix, and real responses from 741 U.S. students. Three open-weight models (Qwen2.5-7B, Mistral-7B, Phi-3-medium) each regenerated the full matrix ten times with sampling and ten times with greedy decoding. Each matrix was fitted with the DINA model, and the study compared the resulting student classifications.

Two sampled runs of the same model, with the same prompt and temperature, assigned different mastery profiles to 61% to 88% of students. Greedy decoding made every run byte-for-byte identical, so the instability came from sampling. It did not fix the disagreement with the expert matrix: 63% to 76% of profiles still differed.

The usual remedy, majority voting across runs, produced the clearest example of the problem. The voted matrix matched the greedy matrix in almost every cell. Yet the few cells that differed changed the diagnosis for half or more of the students.

Kim notes a fair caveat. The expert Q-matrix is not ground truth, and independent statistical validations already flag some of its cells. Model disagreement was somewhat higher on those contested items (0.847 versus 0.659 per item), although the permutation test did not reach conventional significance (p = .060). The constructive reading is that regeneration can act as a screening signal. Items on which models are unstable deserve expert review.

2. Seven Models Agreed With Each Other More Than With the Data

Asking an LLM for an absolute difficulty score has a weak track record. A pairwise question is easier: which of these two items is harder? Michael Chrzan (University of Maryland) and Benjamin Domingue (Stanford) put seven frontier models from OpenAI and Google through pairwise comparisons of 179 items from seven datasets in the Item Response Warehouse, each with respondent-calibrated 1PL difficulties. A hierarchical Bayesian Bradley–Terry model pooled the judgments and estimated each model’s discrimination and its bias toward the first-presented item.

The models were internally consistent. Most showed triplet transitivity above 95%, and agreement across the seven models was high in every dataset (Krippendorff’s α from 0.834 to 0.917). The pooled ranking matched IRT difficulty only moderately (Spearman ρ = 0.400 overall), with dataset values from 0.250 to 0.591.

The authors give the reason plainly: models may converge on salient text features, curricular content, or solution complexity while missing what makes items hard for real respondents. Adding more similar raters can sharpen a shared ordering without correcting a shared construct mismatch. The rater parameters also mattered. Discrimination varied widely across models, and several models showed position bias large enough for the 94% interval to exclude zero. Their recommendation is a limited role for LLM rankings: provisional ordering for item review or rough form assembly, not final calibration parameters.

3. Anchors Help, but Only Inside Their Range

Ummugul Bezirhan and Matthias von Davier (TIMSS & PIRLS International Study Center, Boston College) took a design step that the other studies recommend. Instead of comparing new items only with each other, they compared each new item with calibrated anchor items and fixed the anchors’ known difficulties in the Bradley–Terry model. New items were thus placed on an existing scale.

They used 80 TIMSS grade-4 mathematics trend items as anchors and 101 new TIMSS 2023 items as targets, with the operational 2023 difficulties withheld for validation. A small open model (Qwen3-8B, deterministic decoding) acted as three differently prompted judges and produced 11,607 pairwise judgments. Across all 101 items, the estimates correlated with operational difficulty at r = .484. The authors warn that this figure is inflated, and the breakdown shows why.

Inside the anchor range the relationship was moderate (r = .372). Below it, recovery was essentially zero. The authors conclude that the method interpolates but does not extrapolate, and that the structure of the comparison network may matter as much as the choice of judge. Two other details are useful. The model’s self-reported confidence sat at 0.85 for nearly every comparison, so it carried no information. Its label of “slight” versus “moderate” difference tracked its own certainty, not the size of the real difficulty gap (r = −.02).

4. Similar Wording Did Not Mean the Same Judgment

In formative assessment, the rationale behind a score becomes the feedback. Several studies compare LLM and human rationales with embedding-based cosine similarity. Julie Jung and colleagues (Harvard Graduate School of Education, with clinical partners in Germany) tested whether that number tracks what matters: does the LLM rationale keep the human rater’s central claim?

The data came from a clinical reasoning assessment: 21 medical students, AI-simulated patient encounters, one human rater, and 27 configurations of ChatGPT-5.4 (model size, reasoning effort, and prompt type), yielding 5,790 rationale pairs. A calibrated coding pipeline labelled each pair as fully aligned, partially aligned, or not aligned, and split partial alignment into three subtypes.

Similarity separated clearly misaligned pairs from the rest, but not much more. It could not distinguish full from partial alignment. Within partial alignment, rationales that missed the human’s main point scored as high as rationales that kept it. Length also interfered: the LLM rationale was longer in 89% of pairs, and larger length gaps lowered similarity regardless of meaning. Rationales written in German scored about 0.12 lower than English ones. The authors are careful to say that their codes are not ground truth either. Their point is narrower: similarity alone cannot support a claim that an LLM rationale can substitute for a human one.

The Common Thread

Each study found a property that looked like evidence of quality and was not: identical runs, agreement across models, a strong overall correlation, a high similarity score. In each case the check that mattered was comparison with an external criterion, and in each case the authors had to design that comparison deliberately.

A Reporting Checklist for LLM-Assisted Measurement

These recommendations come from the four papers. I have grouped them so that they can be used when reviewing a manuscript or planning a study.

  1. Report the Full Generation Setup.Model and version, temperature, top-p, top-k, and seeds. Kim found that the effective top-k in his runs was the model’s own default of 20, not the library default of 50 that a reader would assume.
  2. Report Distributions of Downstream Results.Repeat the generation and report how classifications or parameters vary across runs. A single run or a majority vote hides that variation.
  3. Test Stability, Agreement, and Validity Separately.Transitivity, inter-model agreement, and match with response data are distinct properties. Report all three.
  4. Tie Judgments to Calibrated Anchors, and State Their Range.Anchors make LLM judgments interpretable on an existing scale, but estimates outside the anchor range need a separate warning.
  5. Keep Response Data and Human Judgment as the Criterion.Use LLM output to flag items for review or to order a draft pool. Field testing and expert review still set the parameters that support score interpretation.

What This Means for Assessment Work in Indonesia

Teams building item banks and diagnostic tests in Indonesia face the same pressure these papers address: many new items, little pretest data, and limited expert time. LLMs can reduce that load. These studies suggest where they help. They can flag items whose skill mapping is ambiguous, give a provisional difficulty order inside a well-anchored range, and draft rationales that a human then checks for the main claim. They should not replace the expert Q-matrix, the field test, or the human rater as the reference point. All four studies worked with English-language items, or with English and German rationales, so applications to Indonesian items need their own evidence.
