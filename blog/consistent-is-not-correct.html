Consistent Is Not Correct

I read four AIME-Con 2026 papers that put large language models to work on Q-matrices, item difficulty, and rater rationales. They left me with one uncomfortable idea: a stable answer can look a lot like a correct one.

I Nyoman Indhi Wiradika
Published October 10, 2026
10 min read

A post in my LinkedIn feed sent me to the proceedings of NCME’s second AI in Measurement and Education Conference (AIME-Con), held in Pittsburgh on October 5–7, 2026. There are 77 full papers and 60 works in progress, all open on the ACL Anthology. Many of them ask a language model to produce something that then goes into a measurement model: a skill map for each item, a difficulty estimate, a score and its reasons.

I read them as someone who builds tests, so my first question was not about speed or cost. If an LLM’s output feeds a psychometric model, that output is part of the instrument. I would not accept a Q-matrix from an expert panel without checking it. I should not accept one from a model without checking it either.

Three Checks I Now Keep Apart

Reading the four papers side by side, I kept blurring three different claims, so I wrote them down. A procedure is stable if it gives the same result when I run it again. It shows agreement if different models or runs land on the same answer. It is valid for my purpose only if the result matches something outside the model that I already trust: an expert Q-matrix, calibrated item parameters, a human rater’s reasoning. The trap is that the first two feel like evidence for the third. They are not.

1. Same Model, Same Prompt, Different Students

If I could give my students only one of these papers, it would be this one. A Q-matrix states which skills each item requires, and in cognitive diagnosis it decides how a student’s answers turn into a mastery profile. Getting it right is slow, argumentative work, so it is no surprise that several recent studies ask an LLM to draft it from the item text. Most of them report one run, or a majority vote across runs.

Minkwon Kim (Teachers College, Columbia) used the 23 released TIMSS 2011 grade-8 mathematics items, a published seven-attribute expert Q-matrix, and real responses from 741 U.S. students. Three open-weight models (Qwen2.5-7B, Mistral-7B, Phi-3-medium) each regenerated the full matrix ten times with sampling and ten times with greedy decoding. Each matrix was fitted with the DINA model, and the study compared the resulting student classifications.

Two sampled runs of the same model, with the same prompt and the same temperature, gave different mastery profiles to 61% to 88% of students. Nothing about the students had changed. Greedy decoding made every run identical down to the byte, which shows the instability came from sampling. But a stable answer was still not the expert’s answer: 63% to 76% of profiles differed from those based on the expert Q-matrix.

The result I keep coming back to is the majority vote. Voting is the usual fix for unstable runs, and the voted matrix matched the greedy matrix in almost every cell. Yet those few different cells changed the diagnosis for half or more of the students. On paper, the two matrices look the same. For a student receiving a diagnosis, they are not.

To be fair to the models, Kim is careful here. The expert Q-matrix is not ground truth either, and earlier statistical validations already question some of its cells. Model disagreement was somewhat higher on those contested items (0.847 versus 0.659 per item), although the permutation test did not reach conventional significance (p = .060). I find this the most useful part of the paper. Regenerating the matrix many times can work as a warning light: when a model cannot make up its mind about an item, an expert should look at it.

2. Seven Models Agreed, Mostly With Each Other

The title of this paper says it better than I can. Asking an LLM for an absolute difficulty score has a weak track record, so Michael Chrzan (University of Maryland) and Benjamin Domingue (Stanford) asked an easier question: which of these two items is harder? Seven frontier models from OpenAI and Google compared items from seven datasets in the Item Response Warehouse, 179 items in all, each with difficulties already calibrated from real responses. A hierarchical Bayesian Bradley–Terry model pooled the judgments and estimated how sharply each model discriminated and how much it favoured whichever item came first.

By every internal measure, the models looked trustworthy. Most showed triplet transitivity above 95%, and agreement across the seven models was high in every dataset (Krippendorff’s α from 0.834 to 0.917). Their pooled ranking matched IRT difficulty only moderately (Spearman ρ = 0.400 overall), ranging from 0.250 to 0.591 across datasets.

This felt familiar. Put five judges with the same training in a room and they will agree with each other, and still miss what makes an item hard for a fifteen-year-old in a real classroom. The authors say much the same about the models: they may converge on visible features of the text, the curriculum topic, or the number of solution steps, while missing what actually trips up respondents. Adding a sixth similar judge sharpens the shared ordering without fixing the shared blind spot. The rater parameters mattered too. Discrimination varied widely across models, and several showed a position bias large enough for the 94% interval to exclude zero. The authors give LLM rankings a modest job: a provisional order for item review or rough form assembly, not final calibration parameters. I think that is the right job.

3. Anchors Help, Until They Run Out

This is the most hopeful of the four papers, and it also taught me the most about reading a correlation. Ummugul Bezirhan and Matthias von Davier (TIMSS & PIRLS International Study Center, Boston College) did what the other papers recommend. Instead of comparing new items only with each other, they compared each new item with calibrated anchor items and fixed the anchors’ known difficulties in the Bradley–Terry model. The new items were placed on a scale that already existed.

The anchors were 80 TIMSS grade-4 mathematics trend items, and the targets were 101 new TIMSS 2023 items whose operational difficulties were held back for validation. A small open model (Qwen3-8B, deterministic decoding) played three judges with different prompts and made 11,607 pairwise judgments. Across all 101 items the estimates correlated with operational difficulty at r = .484. I would have stopped there and called it moderate. The authors did not, and the breakdown shows why.

Inside the anchor range the correlation was r = .372. Below it, the method recovered essentially nothing. The overall .484 was partly the gap between the two groups, not precision within either. The authors conclude that the method interpolates but does not extrapolate, and that the design of the comparison network may matter as much as the choice of judge. I like this result because it says plainly where the method stops. The items I worry about most in a new bank are often the very easy and the very hard ones, and that is exactly where anchors run out. Two smaller details stayed with me. The model reported a confidence of 0.85 for nearly every comparison, so its confidence told us nothing. And its labels of “slight” or “moderate” difference followed its own certainty, not the real difficulty gap (r = −.02).

4. Similar Words, Different Judgments

As a teacher, I felt this one closest to home. In formative assessment, the reason behind a score is the feedback the student reads. Several studies compare LLM and human rationales with embedding-based cosine similarity. Julie Jung and colleagues (Harvard Graduate School of Education, with clinical partners in Germany) asked whether that number captures what a student would care about: does the LLM’s rationale keep the human rater’s main point?

The data came from a clinical reasoning assessment: 21 medical students, AI-simulated patient encounters, one human rater, and 27 configurations of ChatGPT-5.4 (model size, reasoning effort, and prompt type), yielding 5,790 rationale pairs. A calibrated coding pipeline labelled each pair as fully aligned, partially aligned, or not aligned, and split partial alignment into three subtypes.

Similarity picked out the clearly misaligned pairs and not much else. It could not tell full from partial alignment. Within partial alignment, rationales that missed the human’s main point scored as high as rationales that kept it, which is the opposite of what a student needs. Length got in the way too. The LLM’s rationale was longer in 89% of pairs, and bigger length gaps lowered similarity whatever the meaning. Rationales written in German scored about 0.12 lower than English ones, which made me wonder how Indonesian would fare. The authors are careful to say their own codes are not ground truth. Their claim is narrower, and I agree with it: similarity alone cannot show that an LLM’s rationale can stand in for a human’s.

What I Am Taking Away

In every paper, something that looked like quality turned out not to be: identical runs, agreement across models, a strong overall correlation, a high similarity score. Each time, the check that mattered compared the model with something outside it, and someone had to design that comparison on purpose. That is ordinary measurement work. AI makes it easier to skip, which is why I think it matters more now.

Five Questions I Will Ask Before Trusting LLM Output

I review manuscripts and supervise student projects, and these papers changed what I will ask when LLM output shows up in either. The questions come from the four papers; the wording is mine.

  1. Can I see the full generation setup?Model and version, temperature, top-p, top-k, seeds. Kim found that the top-k actually in effect was the model’s own default of 20, not the library default of 50 a reader would assume. Small settings like this are easy to miss and hard to reproduce.
  2. What happens on the second run?I want to see how classifications or parameters change across repeated runs, not a single run or a majority vote that hides the spread.
  3. Which of the three checks was actually run?Transitivity, agreement between models, and match with response data are different things. A study that reports only the first two has not yet shown validity.
  4. Where are the anchors, and where do they end?Anchors put LLM judgments on a scale I can interpret. Estimates outside the anchor range need their own warning.
  5. What is the criterion?Response data and human judgment should remain the reference. I am happy to use LLM output to flag items or order a draft pool, but field testing and expert review should still set the parameters behind score interpretation.

What This Means for Our Work in Indonesia

Those of us building item banks and diagnostic tests in Indonesia know the pressure these papers describe: many new items, little pretest data, never enough expert time. I understand why LLMs are tempting, and I think they can help. After these four papers, my own line is fairly clear. I would use an LLM to flag items whose skill mapping looks ambiguous, to suggest a provisional difficulty order inside a well-anchored range, and to draft rationales that a person then checks for the main point. I would not let it replace the expert Q-matrix, the field test, or the human rater as the reference. All four studies used English items, or English and German rationales. Before any of this is used with Indonesian items, we need our own evidence, and that is a study I would like to see someone in Indonesia do. Perhaps it should be us.

Consistent is a good start. It is not the same as correct.
