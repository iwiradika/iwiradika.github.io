Behind Indonesia’s PISA 2025 Numbers

Fourteen thousand students represent almost four million Indonesian teenagers. The way PISA turns that sample into national estimates sets the limits of what we can claim.

I Nyoman Indhi Wiradika
Published September 9, 2026
6 min read

The OECD released the PISA 2025 results on September 8, 2026. Indonesia contributed 14,168 students from 419 schools. Together, they represent about 3.995 million 15-year-olds enrolled in school. Each row in the database can therefore stand for a different number of students.

From a Sample to a Population

PISA samples schools, then students within those schools. The OECD assigns each student a weight based on the chance of selection and adjustments for non-response. Analysts use those weights to estimate national means and percentages.

Percentages Say More Than a Rank

Indonesia faces a large shortfall in foundational proficiency. Thirty-seven percent of students reached at least Level 2 in science, 17% in mathematics, and 27% in reading. These percentages describe what students can do. A league-table rank omits that detail.

Why One Student Has Ten Plausible Values

Each participant answers part of the item pool during a two-hour test. PISA combines item response models with questionnaire information to produce ten plausible values. Each value is a draw from a distribution of proficiency that fits the available evidence.

Analysts calculate each statistic ten times and combine the results. Averaging the ten values for each student and treating the result as an individual test score understates measurement uncertainty.

Read the Thin Lines Around the Dots

A different sample from the same population would produce a different mean. The OECD estimates that variation with 80 replicate weights. For achievement statistics, analysts also include variation across plausible values.

Implication for Indonesia

Indonesia should track the share of students reaching baseline proficiency together with its confidence interval. A rank can move after small changes across several systems. A change in baseline proficiency maps onto the number of students who need support.

Digital Access Needs Careful Interpretation

Indonesian students reported 2.5 hours of digital-device use at school for learning, compared with an OECD average of 1.7 hours. Fifty-three percent used AI chatbots to help them learn at least once a week. Those figures capture reported exposure; task quality requires separate evidence.

Three Rules for Reading PISA Results

  1. Pair Every Estimate with Its Interval.A small point difference may disappear after analysts account for sampling and measurement uncertainty.
  2. Start with Proficiency Levels and Trends.Both connect more closely to policy questions than one position in a league table.
  3. Keep Association Separate from Cause.PISA collects cross-sectional data. A correlation among AI use, screen time, and achievement cannot estimate the effect of a policy.

PISA offers a sample-based view of Indonesia’s education system. We preserve that view when we use the weights, plausible values, and uncertainty measures built into the assessment design.
